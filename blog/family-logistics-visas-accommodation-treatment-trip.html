Family Logistics for a Cancer Treatment Trip to Guangzhou: Visas, Accommodation, Food, and What Families Actually Need (2026)

A cancer treatment trip is not just about the patient — it is about the family who travels with them. The patient's oncology plan is only half of what needs to be arranged; the other half is the practical logistics that make the trip possible. Visas — What Each Family Member Needs

Each family member — including minors — needs their own visa. The most common option for a medical trip is a medical-purpose visa, which requires an invitation letter from the hospital or a medical-tourism coordinator.

  • The invitation letter. This is issued by the hospital or the coordinator after the case is accepted. It includes the patient's name, the treatment plan, and the expected length of stay. Mediorient will issue this letter as part of the case-review process.
  • The visa application. Each family member submits their own application at the Chinese consulate in their home country. The processing time is typically 5–10 business days, but it varies by consulate and by whether expedited processing is available.
  • The passport requirements. Each passport must be valid for at least six months beyond the date of entry, and have at least two blank pages. Some consulates require a photo, a visa application form, and proof of accommodation — the invitation letter usually covers the last two.
  • The return-ticket requirement. Some consulates require a confirmed return ticket. This is not always necessary for a medical visa, but it is worth confirming with the specific consulate before you apply.
The timeline: start the visa process as soon as the case is accepted. Do not wait until the treatment date is confirmed — the processing time can be longer than you expect, and an expired passport or a missing document can delay the whole trip.

Accommodation — What a Treatment Family Actually Needs

The best accommodation for a treatment family is usually a serviced apartment within walking distance or a short taxi ride from the hospital. It is not the cheapest option, but it is the option that works — for the reasons below.

  • Location. Within walking distance or a short taxi ride from the hospital. The patient will need to travel to the hospital multiple times per week, and the family will need to be able to get there quickly if there is an emergency.
  • Kitchen facilities. A full kitchen — not just a microwave — so the family can cook their own meals. This is important for patients with dietary restrictions, for halal diets, and for the cost of eating out every meal for three weeks.
  • Cleaning and 24-hour security. A serviced apartment comes with cleaning and security. A hospital guesthouse or a standard hotel does not always have this, and the difference matters when the family is tired and the patient is in treatment.
  • Long-term rates. A serviced apartment at a three-week rate is typically USD $400–$1,100 per week. A hotel at the same location is typically 2–3× more expensive for the same stay.
  • Laundry. A serviced apartment has in-unit laundry. A hotel does not always, and the cost of hotel laundry adds up over three weeks.

Food — Halal, Southeast-Asian, and the Patient's Diet

Guangzhou is one of the best cities in China for international food — and this is a real advantage over many other Chinese cities for Southeast-Asian and Middle-Eastern families.

1. Halal food

Guangzhou has a significant Muslim population, and the halal-food infrastructure is real. There are halal restaurants, halal supermarkets, and halal-friendly serviced apartments. The halal restaurants are concentrated in specific neighbourhoods, and the halal supermarkets are in the city centre. The food quality is good — this is not a token offering.

2. Southeast-Asian food

For Thai, Vietnamese, Indonesian, and Malaysian food, Guangzhou is one of the best cities in China. There are Southeast-Asian restaurants in the city centre, and Southeast-Asian supermarkets carry the specific brands and ingredients families look for. This is a real advantage over Beijing, Shanghai, or Chengdu, where the Southeast-Asian food scene is thinner.

3. The patient's diet

For patients in treatment, the diet is not just about preference — it is about nutrition, appetite, and tolerance. The TCM-oncology team at the larger hospitals includes dietary counselling as part of the recovery track. A serviced apartment with a kitchen lets the family cook meals that match the patient's specific dietary needs, which is a real advantage over eating out every meal.

Interpreters — Why a Medical Interpreter Is Not a Luxury

The larger hospitals have English-speaking coordinators, but a coordinator is not a medical interpreter. A coordinator handles logistics — scheduling, accommodation, travel. A medical interpreter translates the actual oncology conversation — the diagnosis, the treatment plan, the side-effect management, the follow-up schedule.

The difference matters. A patient who does not understand the treatment plan is a patient who cannot consent to it. A family who does not understand the side-effect management is a family who cannot manage an emergency. The medical interpreter is the piece that makes the whole trip possible — and it is not a luxury.

The honest comparison: a medical interpreter costs USD $300–$800 per day. A provider who excludes it from the treatment package is selling a marketing number, not a budget. Mediorient includes the interpreter in the published cost ranges — it is not a separate upsell.

What to Pack — and What to Leave Behind

  • Bring: The patient's medication (with a doctor's letter explaining the prescription), the full imaging in DICOM format on a USB drive, a portable charger, a portable Wi-Fi device (or a local SIM), and a small first-aid kit. The imaging is the single most important thing to bring — the Chinese team will use it to plan the treatment.
  • Leave behind: Large amounts of cash (ATMs are everywhere, and the exchange rate is favourable), heavy luggage (you will be living in a serviced apartment, not a hotel), and any medications that are not essential (you can buy most of them in China, but the brands may be different).
  • Get locally: A local SIM card (available at the airport or the hospital), a local bank card (optional, but useful for large payments), and any specific medications that are harder to find in China (ask the oncologist before you fly).
