English Medical Translation for China Cancer Care: What To Translate, Who Translates It, and Why It Matters (2026)

Google Translate will not save your cancer case. This 2026 guide covers the real medical translation process for a Chinese cancer centre — what to translate, who does it, how much it costs, and the mistakes that delay the treatment decision.

A cancer case in English and a cancer case in Chinese are not the same case to a Chinese senior oncologist. The specialist reads English, but the MDT — the multidisciplinary team that makes the treatment decision — reads Chinese. If the patient brings only English, the oncologist sees the case but the MDT cannot. If the patient brings only Chinese, the coordinator and the specialist cannot. Both versions have to be present, and the translation has to be accurate — not approximate.

This guide covers the real medical translation process for a Chinese cancer centre: what to translate, who does it, how much it costs, and the specific mistakes that delay the treatment decision.

Why Both Versions — Not Just One

The reason for translating into both English and Chinese is not a formality — it is the difference between a case the MDT can read and one they cannot.

Who readsLanguageWhy it matters
The senior oncologistEnglish or ChineseReads the case in consultation
The MDT (multidisciplinary team)ChineseMakes the treatment decision
The coordinatorEnglishHandles logistics and communication
The pathologistChineseReads the original slides and IHC
The radiologistChineseReads the DICOM and the imaging report

The MDT is where the treatment decision is made, and the MDT reads Chinese. If the pathology report is in English only, the pathologist in the MDT cannot confirm the histology. If the imaging report is in English only, the radiologist cannot confirm the extent of the disease. Both versions have to be present — not as a courtesy, but as a requirement for the MDT to make the decision.

What To Translate — The Priority List

1. The pathology report — the highest priority

The pathology report is the document the MDT reads most carefully. It is where the histology, the differentiation, the receptor status, and the molecular profile are recorded. A translation error on this document changes the treatment path.

For the pathology report specifically:

  • The histology terminology must be exact. 'Adenoquamous carcinoma' is not 'squamous cell carcinoma'. 'Poorly differentiated' is not 'low grade'. These are different cancers with different treatments.
  • The molecular markers must be exact. 'MSI-H' is not 'MSS'. 'EGFR exon 19 deletion' is not 'EGFR L858R'. These are different molecular profiles with different drug targets.
  • The immunohistochemistry (IHC) results must be exact. The receptor status (ER, PR, HER2 for breast cancer) is where the translation error is most common — and most damaging.

2. The imaging reports

The CT, MRI, and PET reports should be translated into English (from Chinese if they are from a Chinese hospital) and Chinese (from English if they are from a foreign hospital). The DICOM files — the actual imaging data — are read directly by the radiologist and do not need translation. What needs to be translated is the written report that accompanies the DICOM.

3. The prior treatment history

Every prior regimen — the drug name, the dose, the number of cycles, the response, the reason for stopping. The drug names are the part that is easy to get wrong: 'cetuximab' is not 'panitumumab', 'trastuzumab' is not 'pertuzumab'. A translation error on the drug name can change the treatment plan for the next regimen.

4. The prior specialist reports

If the patient has seen a specialist elsewhere — for example, an oncologist in the patient's home country — the specialist report should be translated into English and Chinese. The report is where the prior diagnosis, the treatment plan, and the reasoning are recorded. Skipping the translation of this document means the senior specialist in China is making a decision without seeing the reasoning of the prior specialist.

Who Should Do the Translation

The translation should be done by a professional medical translation service with oncology credentials — not by the hospital's general translator, and not by an online tool.

Here is why:

1. The hospital's general translator is not an oncology specialist

The hospital has a translation desk, but the translators there are not oncology specialists. They translate what they are told; they do not check that 'MSI-H' is not the same as 'MSS'. For a case where the translation error changes the treatment path, this is not a small risk.

2. Online tools do not know oncology terminology

Google Translate, DeepL, and similar tools do not have oncology-specific terminology. They translate 'adenocarcinoma' correctly, but they may translate 'poorly differentiated' as 'low differentiation' — which sounds the same but is not. For a cancer case, the cost of this error is measured in weeks of ineffective therapy.

3. A professional oncology translator has the terminology

A professional medical translation service with oncology credentials has a terminology bank for oncology — the specific terms, the specific abbreviations, the specific drug names. The translation is checked against the original by a second oncology translator before it is delivered. This is the difference between a translation the MDT reads and one they have to ignore.

The honest cost: a professional oncology translation of a full case package is in the ¥2,000-¥8,000 range. A translation error that changes the treatment path is worth more than the cost of the entire trip. The translation is not an expense — it is the insurance on the treatment decision.

What To Not Translate

  • The imaging DICOM files. The radiologist reads the DICOM directly, not the report. The DICOM does not need translation — the report that accompanies it does.
  • The lab tests in English. If the specialist reads English, the lab tests in English are fine. The coordinator reads English too. No translation needed.
  • The consent forms. The hospital has English consent forms — the patient does not need to translate them.
  • The discharge summary in Chinese. This is produced by the hospital in Chinese and can be translated into English by the coordinator as part of the follow-up plan. It is not needed in English before the patient arrives.

The Common Translation Mistakes

  • 'Poorly differentiated' translated as 'low grade'. These are different things. 'Poorly differentiated' means the tumour cells look very different from normal cells; it is a marker of aggressiveness. 'Low grade' means the tumour cells look similar to normal cells; it is a marker of less aggressiveness. A patient with 'poorly differentiated' cancer who is told they have 'low grade' cancer has been misclassified.
  • 'MSI-H' translated as 'MSS'. These are opposites. 'MSI-H' (microsatellite instability-high) means the tumour has high mutation activity and is likely to respond to immunotherapy. 'MSS' (microsatellite stable) means the opposite — the tumour is not likely to respond. A patient with 'MSI-H' cancer who is told they have 'MSS' cancer will not get the immunotherapy they should.
  • Drug name errors. 'Cetuximab' translated as 'panitumumab' — different drugs, different targets, different side effects. A patient who receives the wrong drug because of a translation error has been harmed.
  • 'Stage IV' translated as 'Stage IVB'. These are different sub-stages with different treatment options. A patient misclassified at the wrong sub-stage may not get the right first-line therapy.
The bottom line: for a cancer case, the cost of a translation error is not measured in words — it is measured in weeks of ineffective therapy. The translation is not a courtesy; it is the insurance on the treatment decision. A patient who skips the professional translation is saving ¥2,000-¥8,000 — and risking the entire trip.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to translate my medical reports into Chinese, or English?

Both — and this is the part most patients miss. The senior oncologist reads English; the MDT (multidisciplinary team) reads Chinese. If the patient brings only English translations, the oncologist can read the case but the MDT cannot — and the MDT is where the treatment decision is made. If the patient brings only Chinese translations, the coordinator cannot read them and the specialist cannot. Both versions should be present.

Can I use Google Translate for medical reports?

No — for anything that drives the treatment decision, no. Google Translate is fine for a menu or a street sign; it is not fine for a pathology report. The specific terms — 'adenoquamous carcinoma', 'poorly differentiated', 'EGFR exon 19 deletion', 'MSI-H' — have exact medical meanings, and a translation error on one of these changes the treatment path. For a cancer case, the cost of a translation error is measured in weeks of ineffective therapy, not in reading time.

How much does a professional medical translation cost?

For a single complex pathology report, it is closer to ¥500-¥2,000 per report. The cost is not the expensive part of the trip; the cost of a translation error is. What documents do I actually need to translate?

The documents that drive the treatment decision: the pathology report (both the histology and the immunohistochemistry), the imaging reports (CT, MRI, PET) in English and Chinese, the prior treatment history (drug names, doses, cycles, responses), and any prior specialist reports. The documents that do not need to be translated: the imaging DICOM files (they are read directly by the radiologist), the lab tests in English if the specialist reads English, and the plain-language consent forms (they have English versions).

Who should do the translation — a professional service or the hospital?

A professional service, not the hospital. The hospital's own translators are not oncology specialists — they translate, but they do not know that 'MSI-H' is not the same as 'MSS', or that 'adenoquamous' is not 'squamous cell'.
