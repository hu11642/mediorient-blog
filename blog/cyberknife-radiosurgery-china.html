CyberKnife Radiosurgery in China: How It Differs from Proton, What It Costs, and Where It Fits (2026)

CyberKnife is frameless, image-guided, robotic radiosurgery — and in China it is a different option from proton, not a worse one. This 2026 guide explains when CyberKnife is the right choice, what the evidence says, what the costs are, and where in Guangzhou you can actually get it.

If you have been researching cancer treatment in China, you have probably read about proton therapy. That is understandable — it gets the most coverage. But for a large share of international patients whose cases would benefit from precision radiotherapy, the more practical option is often CyberKnife: cheaper, more widely available, and with strong evidence in the specific tumour types that show up most often in the chemo-failure pipeline. This guide is about making that distinction cleanly.

What CyberKnife Actually Is

CyberKnife is a robotic radiosurgery platform. It is frameless — the patient is not fixed to a skull-base — and it uses continuous image guidance to track the tumour in real time as it moves with breathing. The radiation comes from many angles through a single robotic arm, which means each pass can be highly focused and the dose to surrounding tissue can be minimised. The result is a treatment that looks like surgery — high dose to a small volume — without the surgical incision.

The clinical terms you will see are SRS (stereotactic radiosurgery) for single-fraction treatments and SBRT (stereotactic body radiation therapy) for multi-fraction treatments. CyberKnife delivers both. The choice between them depends on the tumour, its location, and its proximity to sensitive structures.

The two technologies both deliver high-precision radiation, but the physics and the economics are very different.

The short version: CyberKnife is more available and cheaper for most adult cases; proton has a physics advantage in specific situations (paediatrics, recurrent disease, some skull-base locations). They are not interchangeable — and the right choice is tumour-specific, not 'which is fancier.'

Where CyberKnife Has the Strongest Evidence

1. Early-stage NSCLC

For patients who are not surgical candidates because of age, comorbidity, or remaining lung function, CyberKnife SBRT is now standard of care internationally and in China. The evidence base is strong, and the Chinese protocols mirror international ones.

2. Oligometastatic solid tumours

For liver, kidney, lung, and bone metastases that are limited in number (typically 1–5 lesions), CyberKnife is a well-established option. The 'oligometastasis paradigm' — treating the limited number of sites rather than systemic therapy alone — is now standard oncology thinking, and CyberKnife is the tool that makes it feasible.

3. Spinal and skull-base lesions

For spinal metastases and select skull-base tumours, CyberKnife's real-time tracking is a genuine advantage — the tumour can move with breathing or swallowing, and CyberKnife tracks it. Proton has its own advantages here too; the right choice depends on the specific case.

4. Recurrent head-and-neck, pancreatic, and hepatic disease

For patients who have already had a course of radiation and need a re-irradiation, CyberKnife's precision is what makes repeat treatment feasible without unacceptable toxicity. This is a common scenario in the chemo-failure pathway, and it is where CyberKnife earns its place most clearly.

When CyberKnife Is NOT the Right First Move

  • Widespread metastatic disease. If the tumour is in multiple organs and not oligometastatic, systemic therapy is usually the first move. CyberKnife treats limited disease, not diffuse disease.
  • Tumours very close to critical structures without enough normal-tissue margin. Not every location is feasible for SRS/SBRT — the dose to the surrounding tissue has to be within safe limits, and if it is not, conventional radiation or systemic therapy is the right first move.
  • Purely palliative intent with widespread symptoms. CyberKnife can be used palliatively, but if the goal is symptom control across multiple sites, systemic therapy or conventional radiation is usually more appropriate.
  • Patients who cannot tolerate repeated image-guided positioning. CyberKnife is frameless but still requires multiple fractions for SBRT. For patients whose condition will not hold still, conventional or single-fraction approaches may be safer.

The honest comparison: CyberKnife is not 'proton-lite.' It is a different technology with different strengths. For many adult tumour types, CyberKnife is the right choice on both cost and evidence. For paediatric and some recurrent cases, proton has a real physics advantage. The choice is tumour-specific — not a question of which is more advanced.

How to Verify a CyberKnife Programme Is Legitimate

The CyberKnife market in China has grown fast, and with it, the gap between serious programmes and clinics that are selling the machine has widened. Three checks will separate them:

  • Can they show you the last twelve months of CyberKnife cases for your specific tumour type? Not 'we have used CyberKnife' — cases of the same tumour, same location, same protocol. A serious programme will share anonymised data.
  • What is the radiosurgery team? A CyberKnife course is delivered by a radiation oncologist, a medical physicist, and a trained robotic team. Ask for the specific team, not just the hospital.
  • Will they meet with your home radiation oncologist? A programme that will meet virtually, before you travel, is the one that has a plan to stand behind. A programme that will not is telling you the decision depends on your trust.

What to Bring and What to Ask

Two things matter before any CyberKnife conversation. First, bring your imaging in DICOM format — CT and MRI — so the Chinese radiation oncologist can see the tumour in three dimensions before you fly. Second, ask the MDT for a written treatment plan that specifies the fractionation scheme, the total dose, and the response-monitoring schedule.

The single most useful question to ask any CyberKnife provider: 'Show me the last twelve months of CyberKnife cases for my exact tumour type, and tell me what happened.' If the answer is real — with the outcomes — you are talking to a programme. If the answer is hopeful but vague, walk away.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is CyberKnife different from proton therapy?

CyberKnife is frameless, image-guided, robotic radiosurgery — it delivers high-dose X-ray beams from many angles to a small target, with real-time tumour tracking. Proton uses particle beams with a different dose-distribution physics. CyberKnife is often cheaper and more widely available; proton has a distinct advantage in paediatric and recurrent cases. Neither is universally better.

Which tumours does CyberKnife work for?

The strongest evidence is for early-stage NSCLC, oligometastatic solid tumours (liver, kidney, lung, spine), prostate cancer, brain metastases, and recurrent head-and-neck or pancreatic disease. For large tumours or widespread metastatic disease, conventional radiation is often the better first move.

What does CyberKnife cost in Guangzhou?

An orientation range for a CyberKnife course in Guangzhou is roughly ¥120,000 to ¥300,000 depending on tumour location, number of fractions, and whether it is combined with other modalities. The number is quoted itemised after records review — no bundled mystery packages.

Is CyberKnife the same as stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS)?

CyberKnife is a specific platform for delivering SRS/SBRT. The term 'stereotactic radiosurgery' refers to the technique — CyberKnife, Gamma Knife, and linear-accelerator-based systems all deliver it. CyberKnife is the frameless robotic variant, which is useful for tumours that move with breathing.

Where in Guangzhou can I actually get CyberKnife?

CyberKnife is available at a small number of major centres in Guangzhou — typically tertiary hospitals with active radiosurgery programmes. Not every cancer centre has it, and the machines are expensive. Mediorient will confirm machine availability and protocol track record before any commitment.
