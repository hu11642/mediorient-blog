The Surgical Pathway

Surgery is the definitive treatment for a patient with a localised, resectable tumour. The Chinese surgical team has the highest-volume experience for many cancer types — breast, colorectal, gastric, hepatic, and lung. For a patient with a borderline resectable tumour, the Chinese pathway is neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by surgery — a pathway that is well-established at Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital and Nanfang Hospital.

The Radiation Pathway

1. Stereotactic radiosurgery (CyberKnife, Gamma Knife)

Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) delivers a high dose of radiation to a small target in 1-5 fractions. CyberKnife and Gamma Knife are the two main SRS systems in China. For a patient with a stereotactically accessible tumour — for example, a brain metastasis, a liver metastasis, or a spinal tumour — SRS is a real alternative to surgery. See the CyberKnife guide for the approach.

2. Proton therapy

Proton therapy uses protons instead of X-rays, and the dose is delivered with a different depth profile (the Bragg peak). This is the advantage for a tumour near a critical structure — for example, a brain tumour near the optic nerve, or a lung tumour near the heart. Proton therapy is offered at the top Chinese centres, but the number of proton therapy centres is limited compared to CyberKnife.

3. Conventional external beam radiation

Conventional external beam radiation therapy (EBRT) is the standard radiation modality for most cancer types. It is offered at every cancer centre and is the foundation of the radiation oncology pathway. For a patient with a large or ill-defined tumour, EBRT is the modality of choice.

The Honest Fit Check

  • Right choice for surgery: A patient with a localised, resectable tumour that is accessible to the surgeon.
  • Right choice for radiation: A patient with a stereotactically accessible tumour that is not accessible to the surgeon, or where the surgery would sacrifice too much healthy tissue.
  • Right choice for proton therapy: A patient with a tumour near a critical structure, where the Bragg peak is the advantage.
  • Not the right choice for surgery: A patient with a diffuse or metastatic tumour that is not resectable.
  • Not the right choice for radiation: A patient with a resectable tumour where the surgery is the definitive treatment.
The bottom line: the modality decision is not a formality — it is the decision that changes the patient's outcome. The Chinese MDT is a real second opinion on the modality decision, and it is offered at a fraction of the cost of a Western cancer centre. Frequently Asked Questions

When is surgery the right choice over radiation?

Surgery is the right choice when the tumour is localised, resectable, and the surgical team has the infrastructure for a complete resection with clear margins. For a patient with a single, localised tumour that is accessible to the surgeon, surgery is often the definitive treatment. For a patient with a tumour in a location that is not accessible to the surgeon — or where the surgery would sacrifice a large amount of healthy tissue — radiation is the alternative.

When is radiation the right choice over surgery?

Radiation is the right choice when the tumour is not accessible to the surgeon (for example, a deep-seated brain tumour), when the surgery would sacrifice too much healthy tissue (for example, a central liver tumour), when the patient's performance status does not allow surgery, or when the tumour has spread beyond the resectable field. For a patient with a stereotactically accessible tumour, CyberKnife or proton therapy is a real alternative to surgery. See the CyberKnife guide for the approach.

What is the difference between CyberKnife and proton therapy?

CyberKnife is a frameless, image-guided, robotic radiosurgery system that uses multiple X-ray beams to deliver a high dose of radiation to the tumour. Proton therapy uses protons instead of X-rays, and the dose is delivered with a different depth profile (the Bragg peak). The two modalities are not interchangeable — the choice depends on the tumour location, the patient's performance status, and the clinical team's experience. Both are NMPA-approved in China and offered at the top centres.

The biopsy pathology, the imaging (DICOM on USB — every CT, MRI, PET), the prior treatment history, and the English translations of any prior specialist reports. The imaging is the foundation of the modality decision — the surgeon and the radiation oncologist both look at the DICOM. See the biopsy guide for the process.
