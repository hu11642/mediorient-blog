Clinical Trials in China for International Patients: What's Open, What's Not, and How to Get In (2026)

China runs more oncology clinical trials than any country outside the US — and a growing number are open to international patients. This 2026 guide explains what is actually available, what the eligibility looks like, what it costs, and how to verify a trial programme is legitimate.

China runs more oncology clinical trials than any country outside the US — and a growing number are open to international patients. For families who have exhausted standard options, a clinical trial can be the difference between a treatment and no treatment. But the trial market is also full of providers who over-promise, and the process is different from a Western trial. This guide is about separating the real from the marketing.

How Many Trials Are Actually Running

As of 2026, China has roughly 10,000+ active oncology clinical trials — more than any country outside the US. The distribution is heavily weighted toward immuno-oncology, cellular therapy, and combination protocols, which is where the fastest growth is. The larger centres in Guangzhou — Nanfang Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center, Aix International — run a significant share of these trials, and they are the centres most likely to be open to international patients.

What 'Open to International Patients' Actually Means

Not all trials are open to international patients, and the reason matters:

  • China-only trials. These are testing a Chinese-brand drug against a standard of care, and the protocol is designed for the Chinese population. International patients are typically not eligible. This is the largest category.
  • Global trials with a Chinese site. These are international trials that have a site in China, and international patients can be enrolled at that site. The eligibility is defined by the global protocol, not by nationality. This is where the fit is most likely.
  • China-specific combinations. These are testing a combination that is China-specific — for example, a Chinese-brand PD-1 inhibitor with a Chinese-brand targeted therapy. The protocol is China-specific, but some of these trials are open to international patients if the combination is being tested against a global standard of care.
  • Early-phase trials (phase I/II). These are testing a new investigational agent, and the eligibility is typically narrow — specific mutations, specific stages, specific prior treatment history. The number of patients enrolled is small, and the protocol is the constraint, not the nationality.
The honest answer: the fit for an international patient is in the global trials with a Chinese site, and in the China-specific combinations that are testing against a global standard of care. The rest of the market is China-only by design.

Eligibility — What the Protocol Actually Looks For

The eligibility for a clinical trial is defined by the protocol, and the protocol is what determines access. The most common eligibility criteria:

  • Tumour type and stage. The protocol specifies which tumour types and stages are included. For example, a trial might be for advanced NSCLC with an EGFR mutation, or for advanced HCC within Milan criteria.
  • Molecular profile. Many trials require a specific mutation (EGFR, ALK, ROS1, RET, MET, KRAS G12C, HER2) or a specific biomarker (PD-L1 score, MSI-H, TMB-high). The molecular testing must be done before enrolment.
  • Prior treatment history. The protocol specifies which prior treatments are allowed and which are not. For example, a trial might require that the patient has already failed at least two lines of chemotherapy, or that the patient has not received a specific class of drug.
  • Performance status. The ECOG or Karnofsky score is typically required to be 0–2 (or 0–1 for some trials). This is a hard eligibility criterion — a patient who is too frail to meet the score is not eligible, regardless of the tumour.
  • Organ function. Liver, kidney, and blood function must be within protocol-defined ranges. This is a safety criterion, not a preference.
  • No prior trial enrolment. Most protocols require that the patient has not been enrolled in another active trial in the last 30–90 days, to avoid overlapping exposures.

In China, the travel and accommodation are the same, but the cost of the non-trial alternative is 3–5× lower. The trial itself is a real option — it is not a free ride, but it is a real option.

How to Verify a Trial Programme Is Legitimate

The trial market is full of providers who cannot be separated from a recruitment agency. Three checks will separate them:

  • Can they show you the protocol number and the registered trial on a public registry? A serious trial is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov, the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR), or the WHO ICTRP. A provider who will not give you the protocol number is not running a real trial.
  • Will the trial be conducted at a hospital with a named principal investigator? A serious trial has a named PI — a senior oncologist who is responsible for the protocol at that site. A provider who will not name the PI is not running a real trial.
  • Will they meet with your home oncologist? A serious trial programme will meet virtually with your home oncologist before you travel, to discuss the protocol and the eligibility. A provider who will not is telling you the decision depends on your trust, not on a shared record.

The Risks — and How the Informed Consent Works

The risks of joining a clinical trial are the same as any treatment, plus the risks specific to a trial:

  • The investigational drug may not work. This is the core risk of a trial — the drug is being tested, and it may not be effective for this specific patient. The protocol will explain this before you consent.
  • You may be randomised to a control arm. Many trials have a placebo or standard-of-care arm, and the patient is randomised to one arm or the other. If you are randomised to the control arm, you will receive the standard of care, not the investigational drug.
  • There may be protocol-related events. The protocol defines what happens if the patient develops a specific event — for example, if the patient develops a specific immune-related adverse event, the protocol defines the escalation path. The protocol will explain this before you consent.
  • The trial may be stopped early. If the trial is stopped early — because of efficacy, because of safety, or because of administrative reasons — the patient may be discontinued from the trial, and the follow-up plan may change. The protocol will explain this before you consent.

The informed-consent process in China is the same as internationally — a detailed explanation of the protocol, the risks, the benefits, and the alternatives, with the patient's signed consent before enrolment. When a Trial Is NOT the Right Fit

  • The patient does not meet the eligibility criteria. If the tumour type, stage, molecular profile, or performance status does not match the protocol, the patient is not eligible. The honest answer is to treat with the standard of care, not to force a trial fit.
  • The trial is too far away. If the trial is at a hospital that is too far from the patient's home country, the travel cost and the family logistics may not be justified. The honest answer is to treat locally or to find a trial that is closer.
  • The patient is too frail to meet the protocol. If the patient's performance status or organ function does not meet the protocol's eligibility criteria, the patient is not eligible. The honest answer is to focus on quality of life, not to force a trial fit.
  • The family cannot afford the logistics. If the cost of travel, accommodation, and the interpreter is beyond what the family can afford, the honest answer is to treat locally or to find a trial that covers the costs.

What to Bring and What to Ask

Two things matter before any trial conversation. First, bring your imaging in DICOM format and your molecular testing — the trial eligibility is defined by the protocol, and the protocol is what determines access. Second, ask the provider for the protocol number and the registered trial on a public registry — if they cannot give you the number, they are not running a real trial.

The single most useful question to ask any clinical-trial provider: 'What is the protocol number, and where is it registered?' If the answer is a real number on a public registry, you are talking to a real trial. If the answer is hopeful but vague, walk away.
