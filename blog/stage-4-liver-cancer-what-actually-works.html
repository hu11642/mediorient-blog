The Chinese drug portfolio for advanced HCC is not a discount version of the Western portfolio. It is the same portfolio — the drugs are the same, the sequences are the same, and the evidence base is the same. What differs is the cost, the pace, and the liver-directed toolkit.

The Role of Liver-Directed Therapy

For a patient with limited liver metastases or advanced HCC, the liver-directed toolkit is one of the differentiators at a Chinese cancer centre:

  • TACE. Delivers chemotherapy directly to the liver tumour while blocking its blood supply. A standard pathway for intermediate-stage HCC.
  • HAIC. A catheter delivers chemotherapy directly to the liver, reducing systemic side effects. Combined with systemic therapy, HAIC can convert unresectable liver metastases into resectable disease in a subset of patients.
  • Radiofrequency or microwave ablation. Direct destruction of small liver tumours, usually for tumours under 3 cm.
  • CyberKnife for liver metastases. Stereotactic radiosurgery for a limited number of metastatic liver deposits. See the CyberKnife guide for more detail.

Combined with systemic therapy, these liver-directed approaches can convert unresectable liver metastases into resectable disease in a subset of patients. This is a pathway that is offered at a higher intensity in China than in most Western centres. See the liver cancer guide for the full approach.

The Honest Fit Check

  • Right choice: Unresectable HCC where the atezolizumab + bevacizumab pathway is not available or too expensive locally.
  • Right choice: Advanced HCC where the liver-directed toolkit (TACE + HAIC + systemic) would convert the disease to resectable.
  • Right choice: A patient who has been through prior lines and is facing a second-line decision.
  • Not the right choice: A patient with Child-Pugh C liver function where the trip cost exceeds the benefit.
  • Not the right choice: A patient whose drug path is the same at home and in China.
The bottom line: for stage 4 liver cancer, the honest expectation is not cure — it is 12-24 months of effective disease control at a fraction of the Western cost. That is a real outcome. Frequently Asked Questions

What options are real for stage 4 liver cancer?

For unresectable or metastatic hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the real options are: systemic therapy (lenvatinib or sorafenib as the foundational tyrosine kinase inhibitor, combined with the PD-1 inhibitors atezolizumab, camrelizumab, or tislelizumab), TACE (transarterial chemoembolisation), HAIC (hepatic artery infusion chemotherapy), and for a subset of patients with a limited number of metastases, a combined hepatic resection with ablation. None of these are cures, but they can extend survival by 12-24 months.

What is the role of immunotherapy for stage 4 liver cancer?

For unresectable HCC, the combination of atezolizumab + bevacizumab (the 'Durvaluminab/IMbrave150' pathway) is the international standard of care, and camrelizumab + ribociclib (the Chinese equivalent) is NMPA-approved. The combination response rate is significantly higher than the tyrosine kinase inhibitor alone. This is the pathway that most changes the outcome for a patient who has been through prior lines.

How does China compare to Western care for stage 4 liver cancer?

The drug portfolio is the same — the same TKIs, the same PD-1 inhibitors, the same immunotherapy combinations. What differs is the cost (China is 30-60% of Western cost), the pace (the MDT at a top Chinese centre can move the patient from one line to the next faster), and the liver-directed toolkit (TACE and HAIC are offered at a higher intensity than in most Western centres).

What is TACE and HAIC?

TACE (transarterial chemoembolisation) delivers chemotherapy directly to the liver tumour while blocking its blood supply — it is offered at most Chinese cancer centres and is a standard pathway for intermediate-stage HCC. HAIC (hepatic artery infusion chemotherapy) is a catheter-based chemotherapy delivery that reduces systemic side effects. Combined with systemic therapy, these liver-directed approaches can convert unresectable liver metastases into resectable disease in a subset of patients.

What should I bring to the first consultation?
